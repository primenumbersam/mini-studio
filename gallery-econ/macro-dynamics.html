The Damped Driven Harmonic Oscillator

In classical mechanics and control theory, system dynamics models describe how state variables (stocks) evolve over time via rates of change (flows) governed by feedback loops. When a physical system is displaced from its static equilibrium $x_{eq}$, its trajectory is governed by a second-order differential equation balancing inertia, energy dissipation (damping), and a restoring force:

$$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + c\frac{dx}{dt} + k(x - x_{eq}) = F(t)$$

Normalizing by mass $m$ yields the universal oscillator equation parameterized by the undamped natural frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ and the dimensionless damping ratio $\zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{mk}}$:

$$\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + 2\zeta\omega_0\frac{dx}{dt} + \omega_0^2(x - x_{eq}) = \frac{F(t)}{m}$$
Strict Preconditions for Equilibrium Convergence: For a disturbed system to exhibit reliable mean reversion back to $x_{eq}$, three physical laws must hold simultaneously:
  1. Positive Damping ($\zeta > 0$): Friction must strictly dissipate kinetic energy out of the system.
  2. Time-Invariant Restoring Force ($k > 0$): Hooke's law elasticity must remain intact regardless of displacement magnitude.
  3. Stationary Reference ($dx_{eq}/dt = 0$): The attractor itself must not move in response to the system's trajectory.
02 / Structural Mapping

Physics vs. Financial Market Mechanics

Mainstream neoclassical economics (such as DSGE models) and quantitative mean-reversion strategies implicitly assume financial markets behave like an underdamped ($0 < \zeta < 1$) or critically damped ($\zeta = 1$) physical oscillator. Comparing the parameters exposes why this analogy breaks down:

03 / Why Equilibrium Models Fail

Three Structural Mechanisms Preventing Mean Reversion

1. Negative Damping ($\zeta < 0$) & Positive Feedback Loops

$e^{-\zeta\omega_0 t} \to \infty$

In physics, drag opposes velocity ($F_d = -c\dot{x}$). In financial markets, rapid price velocity ($\dot{x}$) frequently triggers pro-cyclical feedback: rising prices expand collateral values, lower Value-at-Risk (VaR) metrics, force short-covering, and attract momentum chasers. Conversely, falling prices trigger margin calls and forced deleveraging. The damping term flips sign ($\zeta < 0$). Instead of decaying via $e^{-\zeta\omega_0 t}$, the amplitude grows exponentially until the system hits a nonlinear structural boundary.

2. Nonlinear Elasticity Breakdown ($k(x) \to 0$)

Hooke's Limit Violation

Equilibrium economics assumes that the further an asset deviates from fundamental value, the stronger the restoring force ($-kx$) exerted by value arbitrageurs. However, arbitrageurs operate with finite capital and leverage constraints. When displacement $|x|$ exceeds a critical stress threshold $x_{crit}$, market makers withdraw liquidity and value funds face redemptions. The spring snaps ($k \to 0$), eliminating the restoring force entirely and leaving the asset adrift in a new regime.

3. Reflexivity & Endogenous Attractor Drift ($dx_{eq}/dt = \alpha(x - x_{eq})$)

Soros / Hysteresis

In a pendulum, swinging left does not move the ceiling hook to the left. In an economy, prolonged market dislocations directly alter the underlying fundamentals. High equity valuations enable cheap equity issuance and capital expenditure, improving fundamental earnings; prolonged currency depreciation alters domestic inflation and debt sustainability. The equilibrium point $x_{eq}(t)$ chases the state variable $x(t)$. Waiting for reversion to a historical mean results in catastrophic prediction error because the anchor itself has migrated.
